The pattern

The rollout happened. Nothing moved.

Most public organizations are not short on effort or expertise. They're short on the connective structure between a decision and the daily behavior it depends on.

So the mandate gets announced, the sessions get delivered, attendance gets tracked — and six months later the work has quietly reverted. Nobody failed. There was just nothing holding the change in place.

The structure

Nobody buys training. They buy change.

A workshop that isn't wrapped in a change structure ends when the room empties. Anything worth training is worth building that structure around — decisions, messaging, leadership, adoption, follow-through.

So every engagement is built as a change effort first. What gets decided, how it gets communicated, who reinforces it, how it's measured, and what happens when it meets resistance — those are designed before any content is delivered.

That's why the same approach works for a statewide AI rollout and for two nonprofits that can't agree on a shared client intake process. The subject changes. The structure doesn't.

Why this is harder here

Change fails differently depending on who you answer to.

When you are accountable to statute, a change has to survive audit and legislative scrutiny — so it has to be documented as carefully as it is designed. When you are accountable to members, risk tolerance is structurally low, and governance has to lead rather than follow. When you are accountable to a board and funders, the change has to be defensible in a grant report and a board meeting before anyone sees a result.

Most of you are carrying more than one of these at the same time. That is the actual complexity, and it is what a private-sector change playbook does not account for.
